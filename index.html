Most multimodal models see through a pretrained vision encoder, which bundles three design choices: a visual prior from separate pretraining, dedicated vision capacity, and pre-fusion placement of that capacity. We test each choice by training every model from scratch with the same data, backbone architecture, and training recipe, and compare the choices at matched lifetime compute. §2 Setup ↓

What we find

  • The prior is optional. An encoder trained from scratch with the language model is on par with encoders pretrained on the same data. More encoder pretraining raises ImageNet accuracy but not VQA, even when it is free. §3 Visual prior ↓
  • Dedicated capacity is key. Encoder-free models trail on visual understanding even when their backbone gets the encoder's parameters. From 1.7B to 6.4B backbones, they need about 3× the compute to match the encoder's Avg. VQA. §4 Dedicated capacity ↓
  • Vision capacity can sit inside the backbone. Vision-only modules in the earlier backbone layers perform comparably to a pre-fusion encoder. §5 Placement ↓
  • The findings hold beyond lifetime compute. Added to a pretrained 1.7B language model, in-fusion stays comparable to the scratch encoder, while encoder-free falls further behind. In-fusion can also lower time to first token. §6 Beyond lifetime compute ↓

Vision needs its own parameters, not its own pretraining or its own encoder.

§2Setup

What a vision encoder bundles

Most multimodal models acquire visual understanding from a vision encoder pretrained for a separate objective, such as image recognition or image–text alignment. Recent recipes discard parts of that setup. Kimi K3 trains its encoder from scratch with the multimodal backbone, and encoder-free models feed image patches straight into the backbone. Each recipe implies a different answer to what the encoder contributes, if anything at all.

One controlled protocol

Published models differ in data, architecture, training stages, and compute, so comparing them cannot isolate any one choice. Controlled studies of pretrained encoders (McKinzie et al., 2024; Tong et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025) hold the multimodal stage fixed, but they vary which prior an encoder imports rather than whether it needs one. They also treat encoder pretraining as free, though SigLIP 2, for example, trains on 40B image–text samples. We instead run every training stage ourselves, from random initialization.

§3Is separate pretraining needed?→

Separate pretraining is optional

Under a fixed lifetime budget, encoder pretraining competes with multimodal training for compute. We pretrain the same encoder with four objectives (contrastive MetaCLIP, self-supervised WebSSL, and captioning with Cap and CapPa) on the same images, update it during multimodal training, and compare it with the encoder trained from scratch with the backbone.

At 1.7B and 4B, MetaCLIP trails the scratch encoder at every budget, by 1.8 Avg. VQA points at the largest 4B budget.

Takeaway 1: Encoder pretraining offers little advantage. At matched lifetime compute, an encoder trained from scratch together with the backbone is on par with encoders pretrained on the same data.

§4Is vision capacity needed?→

Dedicated vision capacity is key

Encoder-free models drop the encoder and map each image patch directly into the backbone with a linear embedding. Removing the encoder also removes its parameters and changes how raw patches enter the backbone, so a gap could reflect fewer parameters, a weak patch embedding, or missing dedicated capacity.

Across budgets, encoder-free trails by 4.1–6.4 Avg. VQA points at 1.7B and 2.8–6.8 at 4B. To test whether the gap is simply due to the encoder's extra parameters, we give the encoder-free backbone the encoder's 0.4B parameters as extra width, depth, or both.

A richer patch embedding doesn't close it either. Trained on the same 224×224 images, a two-layer projection inspired by Gemma 4 helps, scoring 40.5 Avg. VQA against 39.0 for the linear default, but stays well below the encoder's 44.4.

Takeaway 2: Visual understanding benefits from dedicated vision capacity. Encoder-free models trail the from-scratch encoder on Avg. VQA. Adding backbone capacity with the same encoder parameter budget does not close the gap.

§5Must capacity precede fusion?→

Vision capacity can move in-fusion

An encoder's parameters are dedicated, used only by vision tokens, and pre-fusion, run before vision and text interact. To separate the two, we move the encoder's 0.4B-parameter budget in-fusion, into vision-only modules inside the shared backbone.

Holding the budget at 0.4B parameters, we vary where it goes and which tokens use it.

Placed in the first eight layers, the vision-only FFN branch matches the scratch encoder (45.0 vs. 44.4), while the other placements do no better than encoder-free. Early placement also helps most on OCR and chart questions (36.0 vs. 25.2 for encoder-free), which depend on fine visual detail. With the first eight layers as the default, in-fusion, which also adds vision-only attention projections, holds the same dedicated budget as the encoder.

§5.4Scale

The comparisons hold with scale

We also train the three recipes on a ladder of backbone sizes and token budgets, growing the vision module with the backbone, and fit one law per recipe in model size and training tokens. The figure shows the runs at 64 tokens per parameter.

Caption loss shows the same pattern: encoder-free has higher (worse) loss than the encoder at every size, while in-fusion matches it. On text loss and Avg. Text, the three recipes are comparable.

Takeaway 3: Dedicated vision capacity can move into the backbone's earliest layers. Placed in the earliest layers, vision-only modules with the encoder's budget are comparable to a from-scratch encoder at every backbone size.

§6Beyond lifetime compute

The findings carry over to midtraining, and in-fusion can be faster to serve

Lifetime compute isolates each design choice, but teams often build on pretrained components and care about inference speed. As shown above, a pretrained encoder doesn't help even when its pretraining is free. We also test adding vision to a pretrained language model, and measure time to first token.

Midtraining: adding vision to a pretrained language model

We continue our own 1.7B text-only model from 1T to 1.33T token-equivalents, adding each recipe at equal compute, and include mixture-of-transformers (MoT) for comparison.

MoT with vision-only copies of all 28 layers falls behind MoT on the first eight layers alone, despite 3.5× as many vision-only parameters, consistent with late placement not helping.

Time to first token

A separate encoder must finish each image before the backbone can start, which adds a serial step to inference. We measure time to first token for a 1.7B backbone on one H200 serving 16 concurrent requests, each with one 448-px image and 256 text tokens.

MoT, which copies whole backbone layers for vision tokens, is about as fast as in-fusion up to 0.4B vision-only parameters, but its added time grows faster with the budget, while staying below the encoder's. The encoder-free model is fastest, but at 1.7B and 2.6×1021 FLOPs it trails the encoder by 5.4 Avg. VQA points.

Takeaway 4: Our findings hold beyond lifetime compute, and in-fusion can be faster to serve. A free pretrained encoder still does not help, the comparisons carry over to midtraining, and in-fusion lowers time to first token at comparable VQA.

FAQ

Common questions

Why count lifetime compute?

A pretrained encoder brings compute and data of its own that comparisons usually leave out. SigLIP 2, for example, trains on 40B image–text samples. Counting only the multimodal stage lets a recipe look better just because it spent more in total. Lifetime compute counts every stage that produces the final model: encoder pretraining, any text-only training, and multimodal training. That is the question a team with a fixed budget faces: if a recipe spends 15% of its budget pretraining the encoder, that 15% is not spent on multimodal training. We compare recipes at matched lifetime compute, with budgets from 6.5×1020 to 2.9×1022 FLOPs. The findings don't rest on this accounting alone: a pretrained encoder doesn't help even when its pretraining is free.

How do your encoders compare to released ones like CLIP and SigLIP?

They have the size of a common released encoder but see much less data. Every encoder is a 27-layer, 0.4B-parameter ViT, about the size of SigLIP's So400m, and pretrained encoders see only our own training images, so every recipe learns from the same data. Pretrained four different ways, these encoders are on par with an encoder trained from scratch with the backbone at matched lifetime compute. Our MetaCLIP encoder reaches 80.4% ImageNet linear-probe accuracy after its default pretraining (1.4×1021 FLOPs) and 84.4% after 9.0×1021 FLOPs. With the DINOv2 linear-probe protocol we use, OpenAI's CLIP ViT-L/14 (0.3B parameters) scores 84.3% (Oquab et al.) and SigLIP 2's So400m scores 87.5% (Fan et al.), both at 224 px. Released encoders like SigLIP 2 train on far more data, so our results don't rule out a benefit from the new data they bring.

How is in-fusion different from mixture-of-transformers?

Mixture-of-transformers (MoT) gives each modality its own modules, so the two streams meet only in attention. In-fusion processes every token with the shared weights and adds zero-initialized vision-only weights on top, sized to the encoder's 0.4B-parameter budget, in the first eight layers only. Added to a pretrained 1.7B language model, MoT copies of the first eight layers come close to in-fusion (48.5 vs. 50.1 Avg. VQA). Copying every layer scores 43.3 with 3.5× the vision-only parameters, and it adds 100 ms to the time to first token, compared to 37 ms for in-fusion and 64 ms for the encoder at 0.4B.

Why train from scratch instead of starting from a pretrained language model?

Training every stage ourselves lets us change one design choice at a time on common data and charge each recipe for all of its compute. We also test the midtraining setup, where we continue our own 1.7B text-only model from 1T to 1.33T token-equivalents, adding each recipe at equal compute. There, in-fusion (50.1 Avg. VQA) and the scratch encoder (48.6) stay comparable, and both lead encoder-free (39.8).

Why do other studies find encoder-free as good as an encoder, or better?

Most compare a pretrained encoder with an encoder-free model, as Tong et al. and Tuna-2 do in their unified models, which also generate images. That removes separate pretraining, dedicated capacity, and pre-fusion placement at once, so it can't show which choice matters. Shukor et al. train both designs from scratch, as we do, and find that encoder-free matches or slightly beats the encoder, mainly at smaller sizes. Two choices in their setup work against the encoder. They train it at a reduced learning rate, which in our runs removes most or all of its lead. And they fit scaling laws to validation loss averaged over text, interleaved, and caption data, which is less sensitive to visual processing design choices. In our experiments, using this averaged validation loss separates the encoder and encoder-free by at most 0.05 nats up to 1.7B, while held-out caption loss separates them by up to 0.24 nats and tracks Avg. VQA.
