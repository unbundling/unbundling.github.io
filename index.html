Of the three choices a vision encoder bundles, only dedicated vision capacity keeps its advantage.

Train it jointly with the backbone, and place it early within it.

§2 · Setup

Three choices, bundled

Most multimodal models acquire visual understanding from a vision encoder pretrained for a separate objective, such as image recognition or image–text alignment. Recent recipes discard parts of that setup: Kimi K3 trains its encoder from scratch with the multimodal backbone, and encoder-free models feed image patches straight into the backbone. Each recipe implies a different answer to what the encoder contributes, if anything at all.

One controlled protocol

Published models differ in data, architecture, training stages, and compute, so comparing them cannot isolate any one choice. Controlled studies of pretrained encoders hold the multimodal stage fixed, but they vary which prior an encoder imports rather than whether it needs one, and they treat encoder pretraining as free, though SigLIP 2, for example, trains on 40B image–text samples. We instead run every training stage ourselves, from random initialization.

Common data

  • Text from Nemotron-CC v2 and v2.1
  • Image–text pairs from MetaCLIP 1.2 (English subset)
  • One set of Qwen2.5-VL-32B captions, shared by all recipes

Training schedule

  • Warmup–stable–decay: any checkpoint can be decayed
  • Budgets from 6.5×1020 to 2.9×1022 FLOPs
  • One peak learning rate per backbone size

Lifetime compute

Clifetime = Σs Cs

  • Every training stage counts, encoder pretraining too
  • Recipes are compared at matched lifetime compute

One backbone

  • Dense decoder-only Transformer, trained from scratch
  • Mainly the Qwen3-1.7B and 4B configurations
  • One encoder architecture: a 27-layer, 0.4B ViT

Avg. VQA averages four category means over 16 Cambrian benchmarks, after instruction tuning on Cambrian-7M. Avg. Text does the same over 12 language benchmarks.

§3 · Visual prior→

Separate pretraining is optional

Does the vision encoder need separate pretraining?

Under a fixed lifetime budget, encoder pretraining competes with multimodal training for compute. We pretrain the same encoder with four objectives (contrastive MetaCLIP, self-supervised WebSSL, and captioning with Cap and CapPa) on the same images, update it during multimodal training, and compare it with the encoder trained from scratch with the backbone.

Across backbone sizes, MetaCLIP trails at lower budgets and is never clearly ahead of the scratch encoder. Vision should also start early: in from-scratch training, starting with text only for the first 25–75% of training lowers Avg. VQA by 5 points, even with the same image–text data.

Takeaway 1: Upstream encoder pretraining offers little advantage. At matched lifetime compute, an encoder trained from scratch with the backbone matches or exceeds encoders pretrained on the same data.

§4 · Dedicated capacity→

Dedicated vision capacity is key

Does visual understanding need capacity that only vision tokens use?

Encoder-free models drop the encoder and map each image patch into the backbone with a linear embedding. Removing the encoder also removes its parameters and changes how raw patches enter the backbone, so a gap could reflect fewer parameters, a weak patch embedding, or missing dedicated capacity.

Across budgets, encoder-free trails by 2.4–5.5 Avg. VQA points at 1.7B and 2.8–6.4 at 4B. Is that just the encoder's extra parameters? We give the encoder-free backbone the encoder's 0.4B parameters as extra width, depth, or both.

A richer patch embedding doesn't close it either. Embeddings inspired by Gemma 4 and Inkling help only when they read higher-resolution images: at the encoder's 224 px, a four-layer hierarchical MLP scores 34.5 Avg. VQA against 34.6 for the linear default.

Takeaway 2: Visual understanding benefits from dedicated vision capacity. Encoder-free models trail the from-scratch encoder on Avg. VQA. Adding backbone capacity with the same encoder parameter budget does not close the gap.

§5 · Placement→

Vision capacity can be in-fusion

Must that capacity act before vision meets text?

An encoder's parameters are dedicated, used only by vision tokens, and pre-fusion, run before vision and text interact. To separate the two, we move the encoder's 0.4B-parameter budget in-fusion: into vision-only modules inside the shared backbone.

Where should the budget go, and who should use it? Holding it at 0.4B parameters, we vary both.

Early placement helps most on OCR and chart questions (23.2 vs. 15.4 for encoder-free), which depend on fine visual detail in the raw patch features. With the first eight layers as the default, in-fusion, which also adds vision-only attention projections, holds the same dedicated budget as the encoder on the other side of fusion.

§5.4 · Scale

The comparisons hold with scale

We also train the three recipes on a ladder of backbone sizes and token budgets, growing the vision module with the backbone, and fit one law per recipe in model size and training tokens. The figure shows the runs at 128 tokens per parameter.

With the encoder held at 0.4B instead, the factor stays near 2.6× at 1.7B and 4B. On text loss and Avg. Text, the three recipes are comparable.

Takeaway 3: Dedicated visual capacity can move into the backbone's earliest layers. Placed in the earliest backbone layers, vision-only modules with the encoder's parameter budget are comparable to a from-scratch encoder at every backbone size.

§6 · Beyond lifetime compute

Dedicated capacity still matters in practice

Lifetime compute isolates each design choice, but teams usually build on pretrained components and care about serving speed. A free pretrained encoder still doesn't help (above). Two more settings:

Adding vision to a pretrained language model

We continue our own 1.7B text-only model from 1T to 1.33T token-equivalents, adding each recipe at equal compute, and include mixture-of-transformers (MoT) for comparison.

The full MoT model falls behind despite having 3.5× as many vision-only parameters: what matters is reserving capacity for vision tokens early.

Time to first token

A separate encoder must finish each image before the backbone can start, which adds a serial step to inference.

The encoder-free model is fastest, but at 1.7B and 2.6×1021 FLOPs it trails the encoder by 4.9 Avg. VQA points.

Takeaway 4: Beyond lifetime compute settings, dedicated capacity still matters most for VQA performance. Our findings hold with zero-cost pretrained encoders and in mid-training. Moving vision capacity in-fusion also reduces time to first token with comparable VQA performance.
